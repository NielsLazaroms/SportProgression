# Load progression, effort, rep ranges and starting weights (beginners to intermediates)

Scope note: this research run used about 25 search and fetch calls. Several primary sources (Schoenfeld 2021 full text, the Bell 2023 Delphi full text from one mirror, an arXiv 1RM paper) were blocked or returned as binary, so some numbers come from abstracts or secondary summaries. These are flagged inline. Formatting: "Finding: [Source](URL)".

## 1. Rep ranges: hypertrophy across loads, compounds vs isolations, strength ranges

### Takeaway
Hypertrophy is similar across a wide range of loads (roughly 30% 1RM and up, which is about 5 to 30+ reps) as long as sets end close to failure. Heavier loads (about 1 to 5 reps) give a modest, specific advantage for 1RM strength. Moderate reps (about 6 to 12 or 5 to 10 for compounds, 10 to 20 for isolations) are recommended mainly for time efficiency and comfort, not because they build more muscle.

### Cited Findings
- Schoenfeld, Grgic, Van Every & Plotkin (2021, *Sports* 9(2):32), "Loading Recommendations for Muscle Strength, Hypertrophy, and Local Endurance: A Re-Examination of the Repetition Continuum", argue that the classic heavy/strength, moderate/hypertrophy, light/endurance continuum is not supported. They propose that adaptations can be gained, and sometimes optimised, across a wide spectrum of loading zones: [DOAJ record](https://doaj.org/article/6d51b905504d46f0873bb43d5d84bf21); [bibbase](https://bibbase.org/network/publication/schoenfeld-grgic-vanevery-plotkin-loadingrecommendationsformusclestrengthhypertrophyandlocalenduranceareexaminationoftherepetitioncontinuum-2021)
- Same paper, as summarised in a secondary source: similar hypertrophy across loads of 30% 1RM or more, regardless of age or training status. Moderate loads are the most time-efficient choice because low loads need many more reps: [fisiologiadelejercicio.com summary](https://www.fisiologiadelejercicio.com/recomendaciones-de-carga-en-el-entrenamiento-de-fuerza-para-mejora-de-la-hipertrofia/) (secondary; full text was not retrievable, HTTP 403)
- Same paper: studies often show larger 1RM gains from 1 to 5 reps than from 8 to 12 reps, but this is not universal. The heavy-load advantage goes away when strength is tested on a different modality from the one trained, so the strength advantage is largely skill or specificity: [x-mol abstract](https://www.x-mol.com/paper/1363971354706350080)
- Renaissance Periodization-attributed guidance: 5 to 10 reps develops hypertrophy "incredibly well". The 10 to 20 range suits nearly all movement types, including isolation, barbell and compound exercises: [arvo.guru RP summary](https://arvo.guru/resources/methods/rp-training) (secondary; RP's own primary text was not found)
- Common practical guidance gives isolation work 10 to 20 reps: [arvo.guru rep ranges](https://arvo.guru/resources/rep-ranges) (low-authority, secondary)
- Double-progression guides suggest rep-range widths of about 4 to 6 reps for primary lifts and 6 to 8 for accessories, for example 8 to 12: [setgraph.app](https://setgraph.app/articles/double-progression-the-smarter-way-to-add-reps-and-weight); [alphaprogression glossary](https://alphaprogression.com/en/glossary/double-progression)

### Inferences
- Rule candidates for the coach:
  - Hypertrophy goal: heavy compounds (squat, deadlift, bench, row, overhead press) at 5 to 10 reps; machine or dumbbell compounds at 6 to 12 or 8 to 15; isolations (raises, curls, extensions, flyes, calf raises) at 10 to 20.
  - Strength goal: main lifts at 3 to 6 reps, with accessories still at 6 to 12. Very low rep ranges (1 to 3) are not needed for general gym-goers.
  - Avoid prescribing more than about 30 reps (roughly the 30% 1RM floor), and avoid more than about 15 to 20 on heavy axial compounds such as squats and deadlifts, where technique and cardio fatigue limit the set before the muscle does. This last point is practice-based, not taken from the sources above.
- Because similar growth happens across ranges, an AI can set the rep range from exercise type and equipment (available load increments) instead of goal alone. For example, a lateral raise with 2 kg dumbbell jumps needs a wider, higher range.

### Gaps
- The full Schoenfeld 2021 text (exact zone boundaries) and the Lopez et al. 2021 load meta-analysis could not be retrieved in this run.
- No primary RP, Nippard or SBS document stating the compound 5 to 10 and isolation 10 to 20 split was retrieved. It is widely repeated but here supported only by secondary summaries.

## 2. Proximity to failure (RIR/RPE)

### Takeaway
Hypertrophy improves as sets end closer to failure, but 0 RIR (true failure) gives little or no extra benefit over about 1 to 2 RIR. Strength gains are fairly flat across a wide RIR range. Isolation and machine work can safely go closer to failure (0 to 2 RIR) than heavy free-weight compounds (1 to 3 RIR). People misjudge RIR by about 1 rep, and they judge better near failure and on sets of 12 reps or fewer.

### Cited Findings
- Refalo, Helms, Trexler, Hamilton & Fyfe (2023, *Sports Med* 53:649-665) pooled 15 studies. Failure training had a trivial advantage over non-failure training (ES 0.19, 95% CI 0.00 to 0.37, p = 0.045). With strict failure definitions there was no advantage for momentary failure. Higher velocity-loss thresholds, meaning sets closer to failure, were associated with greater growth. The authors suggest a non-linear relationship: [Deakin repository](https://dro.deakin.edu.au/articles/journal_contribution/Influence_of_Resistance_Training_Proximity-to-Failure_on_Skeletal_Muscle_Hypertrophy_A_Systematic_Review_with_Meta-analysis/22030796); [AUT repository](https://openrepository.aut.ac.nz/items/78679612-6ff4-4d00-95d1-7920d072a760/full). A letter to the editor raised concerns about transparency and some sample sizes: [Biolayne summary](https://biolayne.com/reps/issue-11/do-you-really-need-to-train-to-failure/)
- Robinson, Pelland, Remmert, Refalo, Jukic, Steele & Zourdos (2024, *Sports Med* 54(9)) ran meta-regressions over 55 studies. Strength gains were similar across a wide range of RIR, while hypertrophy improved as sets ended closer to failure. The analysis was exploratory and used estimated RIR, so the exact dose-response is uncertain: [Abertay repository](https://rke.abertay.ac.uk/en/publications/exploring-the-dose-response-relationship-between-estimated-resist/); [News-Medical](https://www.news-medical.net/news/20240801/New-study-reveals-sweet-spot-for-muscle-growth.aspx)
- Refalo et al. (2024, *J Sports Sci*): 18 trained participants (12 men, 6 women), 8 weeks, within-subject legs. Leg press and leg extension were taken to failure on one leg, and to 2 RIR (leg press) and 1 RIR (leg extension) on the other. Quadriceps thickness gains were identical (0.181 vs 0.182 cm). Failure caused more velocity and rep loss, meaning more fatigue: [PubMed 38393985](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/38393985/); [AUT repository](https://openrepository.aut.ac.nz/items/dbe641c7-a606-4e00-94bc-bb2fbe253afa)
- Halperin et al. (2022, *Sports Med* 52:377-390), a scoping review and exploratory meta-analysis of 12 studies with 414 participants. People underpredicted reps to failure by about 0.95 reps (95% CI 0.17 to 1.73), with very high heterogeneity (I² = 97.9%). Accuracy was better when predictions were made closer to failure and in sets of 12 reps or fewer, and slightly better in later sets. Training status did not clearly affect accuracy: [SportRxiv preprint](https://sportrxiv.org/index.php/server/preprint/view/67); [BVS/PubMed record](https://pesquisa.bvsalud.org/ses/resource/pt/mdl-34542869)
- Practitioner guidance varies: "0 to 3 RIR generally effective" for compounds and isolations; "1 to 3 RIR heavy compounds, 0 to 2 RIR machines and isolations"; some say 0 to 1 RIR for curls, raises and leg extensions: [arvo.guru RP summary](https://arvo.guru/resources/methods/rp-training); [davidveksler cheatsheet](https://cheatsheets.davidveksler.com/strength-training.html) (secondary)
- Helms et al. (2018, *Front Physiol*): 21 trained men, 8 weeks of DUP squat and bench. RPE-based load selection and %1RM loading gave similar strength gains. Squat +17.05 vs +13.91 kg, bench +10.70 vs +9.64 kg, RPE vs %1RM, with no significant difference. RPE "may provide a small 1RM strength advantage in a majority of individuals": [Frontiers](https://www.frontiersin.org/articles/10.3389/fphys.2018.00247/text)

### Inferences
- Rule candidates:
  - Default target 1 to 2 RIR on the last working set. Heavy barbell compounds 2 to 3 RIR. Isolation and machine work 0 to 2 RIR, with failure on the last set allowed.
  - Do not plan true failure on squats, deadlifts or other heavy free-weight compounds.
  - For beginners, because RIR estimates are about 1 rep off and worse far from failure, use rep targets plus "stop when form breaks or bar speed slows a lot". Treat a logged set that clearly undershot the rep target (failed reps) as about 0 RIR.
  - Logged sets that land well above the range top (for example 15 reps when 8 to 12 was the target) imply the RIR was far higher than intended. That means the load was too light, so increase it more aggressively.
- Since the app logs reps x kg only (no RIR), the AI can infer effort from rep drop-off across sets at the same load. A large drop (for example 12, 9, 7) suggests sets near failure. Flat reps (10, 10, 10) suggest RIR of 2 or more.

### Gaps
- No direct evidence on how well complete beginners (first weeks) judge RIR. Halperin found no training-status effect, but data are heterogeneous.
- No controlled comparison of compounds vs isolations for optimal RIR. The split is practitioner consensus.

## 3. Progression models and concrete rules

### Takeaway
Beginners can add load every session (linear progression) until repeated stalls. Double progression (fixed range, add reps, then add the smallest available weight once every set hits the top of the range) is the most broadly usable rule for general gym-goers and for every exercise type. RPE-based autoregulation performs as well as or slightly better than %1RM for trained lifters. Percentage systems like 5/3/1 use a training max of 85 to 90% of 1RM and add fixed amounts per 3 to 4 week cycle. Stalls are handled by repeating, then resetting load 5 to 10% or changing the rep range.

### Cited Findings
- Linear progression: weight increases every workout in small increments, and a sound program should give several weeks of clean progress before the first serious stall: [Barbell Logic](https://barbell-logic.com/linear-progression-the-simple-way-to-strength/); [brace-ai glossary](https://brace-ai.pages.dev/glossary/linear-progression/)
- Starting Strength reset rule: a reset is a 5 to 10% drop in training loads without changing the programming. After a layoff, reset 5% if under 40 and 10% if over 40. After 3 to 4 weeks off, reset 10% regardless: [Starting Strength, "The Reset: Why and How"](https://mail.startingstrength.com/article/the-reset-why-and-how)
- Linear progression is considered exhausted after repeated stalls, form breakdown or recovery problems that persist after repeating the weight, using smaller jumps or resetting. The next step is double progression or an intermediate program: [progressiverehabandstrength.com](https://progressiverehabandstrength.com/articles/barbell-training-reset); [rippedbody, training plateaus](https://rippedbody.com/training-plateaus/)
- Double progression: keep the load fixed and add reps up to the top of the range, then increase load and rebuild. The strict version requires every working set to hit the top before load goes up. If the new load cannot reach the bottom of the range, drop back about 5 lb (about 2.5 kg): [alphaprogression](https://alphaprogression.com/en/glossary/double-progression); [setgraph.app](https://setgraph.app/articles/double-progression-the-smarter-way-to-add-reps-and-weight)
- Typical increments in double-progression guides: 2.5 to 5 lb (about 1 to 2.5 kg) for upper-body lifts and 5 to 10 lb (about 2.5 to 5 kg) for lower-body lifts: [setgraph.app](https://setgraph.app/articles/double-progression-the-smarter-way-to-add-reps-and-weight) (secondary)
- 5/3/1 (Wendler): training max (TM) is 90% of 1RM in the original, 85% in many later variants. After each 3-week wave (plus a deload week in classic versions), add 5 lb (about 2.5 kg) to bench and press TM and 10 lb (about 5 kg) to squat and deadlift TM. Some sources say these increments apply regardless of AMRAP. Wendler later recommends periodic TM test weeks and lowering the TM if it is too high: [Lift Vault TM definition](https://liftvault.com/resources/531-training-max-definition-examples/); [thefitness.wiki 5/3/1 primer](https://thefitness.wiki/5-3-1-primer/); [Liftosaur](https://liftosaur.com/programs/the5314b); [Powerlifting to Win, Beyond 5/3/1 review](https://www.powerliftingtowin.com/?p=1744)
- RPE-based autoregulation vs %1RM gave equivalent or slightly better strength gains in trained men (Helms 2018, see section 2): [Frontiers](https://www.frontiersin.org/articles/10.3389/fphys.2018.00247/text)

### Inferences
- Proposed rule set for the AI coach, built from the cited elements:
  1. Classify the lifter per exercise. "Linear" if they are new to the lift or still adding load almost every session. Otherwise "double progression".
  2. Linear (beginner compounds): if all working sets hit target reps (for example 3x5 or 3x8), add load next session. Use +2.5 kg on upper-body barbell lifts (or +1 to 1.25 kg if microplates exist), +2.5 to 5 kg on squat, and +5 kg on deadlift early on. If target reps are missed, repeat the load. After missing at the same load twice or three times in a row, reset 5 to 10% and climb again, or switch that lift to double progression.
  3. Double progression (default for intermediates and all isolations): use the rep range from section 1. If every working set reached the range top at the target effort, increase by the smallest available increment: about 2.5 kg barbell, the next dumbbell (usually +2 kg total per hand, sometimes 2.5 kg), or the next machine pin. Then expect reps to fall to around the bottom of the range. If reps after the jump fall below the range bottom, go back to the previous load and add reps, or use a smaller jump.
  4. Small-muscle isolations (lateral raise, curls, rear delts): a dumbbell jump can be 20 to 25% of the load (for example 8 to 10 kg). So require the top of a wide range (for example 12 to 20) before jumping, and allow the reps-first path to run longer.
  5. Stall handling (no rep or load progress for 2 to 3 exposures, or reps falling): first check for missed sleep or a layoff and repeat. Then reduce the load 5 to 10% and rebuild. Then change the rep range (for example 6 to 8 to 10 to 12) or swap the variation.
  6. After a layoff: up to about 2 weeks, resume at the same or about 5% lower load. For 3 to 4 weeks or more, about 10% lower (Starting Strength reset guidance).
- A percentage-based scheme (5/3/1 TM at 85 to 90%) is better offered as an optional template than as the AI's default. It needs a 1RM or e1RM per lift.

### Gaps
- No primary SBS, Juggernaut or GZCL texts were retrieved in this run. Juggernaut Method (wave rep maxes) and GZCL (tiered T1/T2/T3 with rep-target progressions) rules are therefore not documented here.
- No research-based percentage increment rule (for example "2 to 5% upper, 5 to 10% lower") was retrieved from a primary source. The kg increments above are practitioner convention.

## 4. Deloads

### Takeaway
Deload evidence is thin and recent. A full week off at mid-block did not hurt hypertrophy but slightly blunted strength in trained lifters. A reduced-volume deload in untrained men did not hinder gains. Practitioners deload about every 5 to 6 weeks for about a week, mostly by cutting sets and effort (RIR) while keeping some load. Expert consensus supports planned, reactive or combined deloads but gives no fixed percentages.

### Cited Findings
- Bell et al. (2023, *Sports Med Open* 9:87), international Delphi consensus (34 coaches in round 1, 21 by round 3, 70% or more agreement threshold). Deloading is defined as "a period of reduced training stress designed to mitigate physiological and psychological fatigue" and to improve readiness for the next block. Consensus (100%) items: reduce sets, reps and training days; shorten sessions; reduce proximity to failure; deload frequency should depend on the athlete's response; pre-planned, autoregulated and combined approaches are all acceptable. There was no consensus on duration in days, percentage volume reduction or %1RM. The "every 4 to 6 weeks, about 7 days" figures in the paper come from its literature review, not panel agreement: [PMC10511399](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10511399/)
- Bell et al. (2024, *Sports Med Open*), survey of 246 strength and physique athletes. They deload every 5.6 ± 2.3 weeks (range 1 to 12), for 6.4 ± 1.7 days (range 1 to 14). 78.9% reduce weekly sets and 52.8% reduce reps per set. 83.7% reduce load on multi-joint lifts vs 60.2% on single-joint. 84.9% reduce effort (RPE/RIR) on multi-joint vs 61.8% on single-joint. Timing: 47.2% pre-planned, 39.4% combined, 13.4% reactive only. 92.3% deload to reduce fatigue. The authors suggest active deloads that keep frequency and exercise selection may be preferable to full breaks: [PMC10948666](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10948666/)
- Coleman et al. (2024, *PeerJ*): 39 trained adults on a 9-week high-volume program (5 sets of 8 to 12 RM to failure, about 90 weekly sets), with a full 1-week training break in week 5 vs continuous training. Hypertrophy, power and endurance were similar. Squat 1RM rose 92.8 to 105.8 kg (break) vs 95.9 to 112.3 kg (continuous), with posterior probability 0.85 favouring continuous. Isometric strength posterior probability was 0.92 favouring continuous. This tested full cessation, not a reduced-load deload: [PMC10809978](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10809978/); [PeerJ](https://peerj.com/articles/16777)
- Pancar et al. (2026, *Sci Rep*): reducing weekly sets and frequency at the midpoint and end of an 8-week program did not hinder hypertrophy or strength-endurance in untrained young men. It did not test load-reduced or self-regulated deloads, or trained people (as reported via search summary): [sci-sport.com summary](https://sci-sport.com/en/?p=95335) (secondary)

### Inferences
- Rule candidates:
  - Beginners (first roughly 3 to 6 months, still progressing linearly) rarely need scheduled deloads. Use reactive ones.
  - Intermediates: offer a deload about every 4 to 8 weeks, or reactively when two or more lifts stall or regress in the same week, or when the user reports joint pain or poor sleep.
  - Deload design: keep the same exercises and frequency. Cut working sets by about 40 to 50% (for example 4 sets to 2) and keep load the same or about 10% lower, ending sets at 3 to 4 or more RIR. Last for 5 to 7 days.
  - Avoid recommending a full week off as the default, since Coleman 2024 suggests it can cost strength.
- The 40 to 50% set reduction and about 10% load figures are practitioner convention consistent with the survey and consensus direction. They are not evidence-derived numbers.

### Gaps
- No trial comparing deload types (volume vs intensity reduction) in trained lifters was found.
- The Bell Delphi paper's full text beyond the first 100k characters was not read. Possible additional numeric guidance in the later sections is unchecked.

## 5. Starting weights for a new exercise

### Takeaway
No strong research exists on picking a first-session load. Practical methods are: start conservatively and ramp within the session (warm-up sets toward a target RIR), estimate from a known related lift using rough ratios, or estimate from an e1RM formula. e1RM formulas are reasonable at 10 reps or fewer and lose accuracy above about 10 reps, especially on lower-body lifts.

### Cited Findings
- Epley (1RM = w × (1 + reps/30)) and Brzycki (1RM = w × 36 / (37 − reps)) perform similarly for 1 to 10 reps: [fitnessvolt e1RM formula comparison](https://fitnessvolt.com/rpe-training/compare/e1rm-formulas/) (secondary)
- Prediction accuracy drops at higher reps. LeSuer et al. (1997, 67 untrained students) found better accuracy for sets under 10 reps. Reynolds et al. (2006) found better leg press and chest press predictions from 5RM loads than from 10RM or 20RM loads: [Journal of Human Kinetics (2022) paper citing them](https://johk.pl/wp-content/uploads/2023/04/10078-82-2022-v82-2022-18.pdf); [PMC9465738](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9465738)
- All analysed equations overestimated 45° leg press 1RM by 6 to 15%: [RBAFS (Brazilian study)](https://rbafs.org.br/RBAFS/article/download/2400/pdf47/3542)
- A 2026 arXiv preprint fitted a "weight-dependent" 1RM equation on 303,494 near-failure sets across 388 exercises. Its contents could not be extracted (binary PDF): [arXiv 2603.17495](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2603.17495)
- Unsourced community ratio table (back squat = 100%): front squat 85%, bench 75%, close-grip bench 67.5%, incline bench 60%, standing press 45%, push press 63.75%, supported row 52.5%, deadlift 120%, weighted dip (total) 78.75%, pull-up (total) 67.5%, standing curl 26.25%. No source is given, and it uses Olympic-lifting-style norms: [myratios Google Site](https://sites.google.com/view/myratios/home/)
- Dumbbell bench per hand is about 40 to 45% of barbell bench 1RM (FitnessVolt about 40%; Lyfta: 100 kg bench corresponds to about 42.5 kg DB bench). Ratios depend on which implement the person trains: [fitnessvolt bench vs DB bench](https://fitnessvolt.com/strength-standards/compare/bench-press-vs-dumbbell-bench-press/); [lyfta comparison](https://lyfta.app/tools/exercise-comparison/bench-press-7-vs-dumbbell-bench-press-6hh); [gunsmithfitness](https://gunsmithfitness.com/blogs/news/how-much-less-can-you-dumbbell-press-compared-to-bench)
- Strength Level publishes exercise-vs-exercise comparison tables built from user-submitted lifts (for example front squat vs incline bench, incline DB bench vs bench). A male intermediate front squat of 232 lb vs incline bench of 196 lb was reported: [Strength Level front squat vs incline bench](https://strengthlevel.com/strength-standards/front-squat-vs-incline-bench-press); [Strength Level incline DB bench vs bench](https://strengthlevel.com/strength-standards/incline-dumbbell-bench-press-vs-bench-press)

### Inferences
- Rule candidates for a never-done exercise:
  1. If a related lift exists in the log, compute its e1RM from the best recent set of 10 reps or fewer (Epley). Multiply by a conservative ratio (front squat about 0.8 of back squat; incline barbell about 0.8 of flat bench; DB bench per hand about 0.4 of barbell bench, incline DB slightly less; overhead press about 0.6 of bench, derived from 45/75 in the table above). Then take about 70 to 75% of that estimated 1RM as the first working weight for 8 to 12 reps. This leaves RIR buffer for technique learning and ratio error.
  2. If nothing related exists, prescribe "find the working weight": start with an empty bar, the lightest dumbbell or a low machine pin. Do sets of about 8 to 10, adding a step each set until the set feels about 3 RIR. Log that as the working weight, and the next session starts double progression from there.
  3. First-session target effort: 2 to 3 RIR, not failure, because RIR judgement is poor on unfamiliar movements and higher-rep sets.
- Do not use e1RM from sets above about 12 reps to set heavy loads. Prefer the 3 to 10 rep sets in the log, and discount lower-body estimates.

### Gaps
- No peer-reviewed table of inter-exercise ratios (for example RDL vs deadlift, lateral raise vs overhead press) was found. The available ratios are unsourced community tables or user-submitted aggregates (Strength Level, Symmetric Strength). Symmetric Strength's own ratio data was not retrieved.
- The 2019 JSCR meta-analysis claimed by blogs (Epley/Mayhew best at 6 to 15 reps) could not be verified.
- RDL vs deadlift and lateral raise vs overhead press ratios: no source found.

## 6. Beginner vs intermediate differences

### Takeaway
Beginners progress session to session on linear models for several weeks to months. Intermediates progress weekly or per cycle and benefit from rep-range or RPE-based schemes and planned or reactive deloads. Quantitative progression rates by training age were not found in this run.

### Cited Findings
- Linear (session-to-session) progression should give several weeks of clean progress before the first stall. After repeated stalls despite repeats, smaller jumps and resets, the lifter moves to an intermediate approach: [Barbell Logic](https://barbell-logic.com/linear-progression-the-simple-way-to-strength/); [progressiverehabandstrength.com](https://progressiverehabandstrength.com/articles/barbell-training-reset)
- 5/3/1-style per-cycle increments (+2.5 kg upper / +5 kg lower per roughly month) represent intermediate-pace progression: [thefitness.wiki 5/3/1 primer](https://thefitness.wiki/5-3-1-primer/)
- Untrained men in Pancar 2026 tolerated reduced-volume deloads without losing gains, and the deload survey population was competitive athletes. Deload needs likely scale with training age: [sci-sport.com](https://sci-sport.com/en/?p=95335); [PMC10948666](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10948666/)

### Inferences
- Expected progression pace for rules: beginner compounds add load each session (or each week for isolations). Intermediate compounds gain about 1 to 2.5 kg per week to per month. If a beginner's lift stalls three sessions in a row, switch that lift to double progression rather than continuing linear resets indefinitely.

### Gaps
- No study quantifying typical %/week strength gains by training status was retrieved (for example from SBS or meta-analyses).

## 7. Bodyweight exercises (pull-ups, dips): reps, then added weight, assisted variants

### Takeaway
Practical guidance (non-peer-reviewed): build reps at bodyweight to about 8 to 12 clean reps over 3 or more sets, then add small external load (2.5 to 5 kg) and drop to about 5 to 8 reps. Below 1 to 5 strict reps, use band or machine assistance and negatives, reducing assistance as reps rise.

### Cited Findings
- Add load once you can do 3 to 5 sets of 8 to 12 clean pull-ups. With more than 12 bodyweight reps, start very light for 2 to 4 sets of about 8 and work up to 12 or more before adding again. A first increment of 2.5 to 5 kg via belt or vest is typical, or add 2.5 to 5 lb after hitting 3x8 for strength-oriented work: [Bullbar Fit progression guide](https://bullbarfit.com/blogs/updates/weighted-pull-ups-built-like-a-strength-program-a-progression-guide-that-spares-your-elbows); [Bullbar Fit safe weighted start](https://bullbarfit.com/blogs/q-as/how-to-safely-progress-to-doing-weighted-pull-ups) (equipment-seller sources)
- Band-assisted exit criterion: 3x8 to 10 with a light band (about 15 to 25 lb assistance) at full range, then lighter bands plus negatives. One unverified claim says the first unassisted rep takes 8 to 16 weeks of structured work: [snuggym pull-up progressions](https://snuggym.pages.dev/programming-workouts-pull-up-progressions-guide) (low authority)
- The community ratio table gives weighted dip total (bodyweight plus added load) at about 79% and pull-up total at about 68% of back squat 1RM: [myratios](https://sites.google.com/view/myratios/home/) (unsourced)

### Inferences
- Rule candidates:
  - Treat bodyweight exercises with double progression on reps first, using a rep range of 6 to 12 (pull-ups, chin-ups, dips) or 8 to 20 (push-ups, inverted rows).
  - When all sets hit the top, suggest +2.5 kg (or +5 kg for dips) on a belt and drop to the range bottom.
  - For assisted machines, log assistance as negative load. Progress by reducing assistance one pin or band level when all sets reach the top of the range.
  - Store the user's bodyweight so total system load can be used for e1RM and ratios.
- For logging (reps x kg), bodyweight sets may be logged as 0 kg. The AI should treat a 0 kg pull-up as bodyweight, not as an empty load.

### Gaps
- No peer-reviewed evidence on bodyweight progression thresholds was found. All guidance here is practitioner or equipment-vendor content.
