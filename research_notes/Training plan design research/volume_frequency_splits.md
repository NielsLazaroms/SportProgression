# Training volume, frequency and weekly split design (hypertrophy and strength, beginners to intermediates)

Note on separators: this file avoids em/en dashes; ranges use a plain hyphen (e.g. 12-20).

## 1. Weekly set ranges per muscle group, and how to count indirect volume

### Takeaway
The best current dose-response data (Pelland et al., 67 studies, 2,058 participants) show hypertrophy keeps rising with weekly sets but with diminishing returns: about 4 fractional sets/week is the minimum detectable dose, 5-10 is the most efficient zone, 11-18 is still clearly productive, and 19-29+ still adds growth but needs ever more sets per detectable gain. Strength plateaus far earlier (about 4-5 fractional sets per lift/muscle per week). Count indirect sets as 0.5 ("fractional" counting), which fit the data best.

### Cited Findings
- Pelland, Remmert, Robinson, Hinson, Zourdos (SportRxiv preprint v2, 2024; later published in Sports Medicine): 67 studies, 2,058 participants; Bayesian meta-regressions adjusted for duration and training status. Posterior probability that the volume slope is > 0 was 100% for both hypertrophy and strength; diminishing returns were "considerably more pronounced" for strength. — [SportRxiv 460](https://sportrxiv.org/index.php/server/preprint/view/460); [Springer PDF](https://link.springer.com/content/pdf/10.1007/s40279-024-02006-3.pdf)
- Counting method: indirect sets (exercises not specific to the measured muscle/lift) were weighted 1 ("total"), 0.5 ("fractional") or 0 ("direct"). The fractional method had the strongest relative evidence and was used for the primary models. Practical meaning: a bench press set counts as 1 chest set and 0.5 triceps set; a row counts as 1 back set and 0.5 biceps set. — [SportRxiv 460](https://sportrxiv.org/index.php/server/preprint/view/460)
- Volume efficiency tiers for hypertrophy (fractional weekly sets per muscle; SDES = smallest detectable effect, 2.05% muscle size), from Table 2A of the preprint full text: — [SportRxiv 460 full text PDF](https://sportrxiv.org/index.php/server/preprint/download/460/967/908)
  - Minimum effective dose: 4 sets/week
  - Higher efficiency: 5-10 sets (about 6 extra sets needed per further detectable gain)
  - Intermediate efficiency: 11-18 sets (about 8.5 extra sets per detectable gain)
  - Lower efficiency: 19-29 sets (about 10.75 extra sets per detectable gain)
  - Lowest efficiency: 30-42 sets (about 12.5 extra sets per detectable gain)
  - 43+: unclear (insufficient data, possibly less growth)
  - Authors: "no clear plateau" in hypertrophy, but caution because few studies tested about 25+ fractional sets/week.
- Volume tiers for strength (SDES = 3.96% 1RM), Table 2B: minimum effective dose 1 set/week; 2 sets higher efficiency; 3-4 sets intermediate; 5+ sets "do not consistently enhance strength gains" beyond the SDES (functional plateau, reciprocal model). — [SportRxiv 460 full text PDF](https://sportrxiv.org/index.php/server/preprint/download/460/967/908)
- Marginal slope at the mean volume (about 12.25 fractional sets/week): +0.24% muscle size per extra weekly set (95% CrI 0.15-0.33%); Schoenfeld et al. 2017 reported +0.38% per extra set using a linear model. — [SportRxiv 460 full text PDF](https://sportrxiv.org/index.php/server/preprint/download/460/967/908)
- Typical study volumes in the dataset: hypertrophy median 10.5 fractional sets/week (mean 13.0 ± 8.9), frequency median 2 sessions/week (mean 2.33); strength median 6 sets/week (mean 8.1 ± 6.2). So evidence is densest in the 6-20 set range. — [SportRxiv 460 full text PDF](https://sportrxiv.org/index.php/server/preprint/download/460/967/908)
- Schoenfeld, Ogborn & Krieger 2017 (dose-response meta-analysis) found greater growth, non-significantly (p = 0.076), in groups doing ≥9 sets/muscle/week vs <9 (ES 0.46 vs lower); commonly summarised as "10+ weekly sets per muscle". Earlier literature suggested substantial diminishing returns beyond about 12-20 sets/week. — cited within [SportRxiv 460 full text PDF](https://sportrxiv.org/index.php/server/preprint/download/460/967/908)
- Baz-Valle et al. 2022 (systematic review, trained men): concluded 12-20 weekly sets per muscle group "may be an optimum standard recommendation" for hypertrophy in young trained men. Secondary sources disagree on whether >20 sets showed a favourable trend or worse results. — [Baz-Valle 2022, PMC](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8884877)
- Renaissance Periodization (Israetel) volume landmarks: MV (maintenance) about 6 working sets/muscle/week when trained at least 2x/week; MEV = lowest volume that produces growth and the start point of a mesocycle; MAV = productive range between MEV and MRV that rises over the mesocycle; MRV = the most volume that can be recovered from. RP gives 18 direct sets/week as an example triceps MRV. RP's own page does not list a full per-muscle table. — [RP: Training Volume Landmarks](https://rpstrength.com/blogs/articles/training-volume-landmarks-muscle-growth)
- RP-style progression: start a mesocycle near MEV, add sets weekly, deload as MRV approaches. — [RP: In Defense of Set Increases](https://rpstrength.com/blogs/articles/in-defense-of-set-increases-within-the-hypertrophy-mesocycle); [Arvo summary](https://arvo.guru/resources/methods/rp-training)
- Third-party summaries of RP landmarks (not primary, inconsistent): chest and quads MEV 6-8 to MRV 20-24; back MEV 8-10 to MRV 22-26; quads also given as MEV 8-10, MAV 12-18, MRV 20-25; hamstrings MEV 6-10, MAV 10-16, MRV 20; front delts rarely need direct work because pressing covers them; biceps 4-6 direct sets as a maintenance baseline, at least 8 for intermediates. — [Arvo volume landmarks](https://arvo.guru/resources/volume-landmarks); [Arvo calculator](https://arvo.guru/de/tools/volume-calculator); [Hypertrophy Tower](https://hypertrophytower.pages.dev/hypertrophy/weekly-volume); [biceps memory-cue doc](https://docs.google.com/document/d/1dnKu8QX4jrdJbPL7HC11bofZMu8EqfVM1L6y5khKhmM/export?format=pdf)
- Jeremy Ethier (Built With Science): target about 10-20 sets/muscle/week; beginners about 10; arms, traps and delts get indirect volume from compounds so direct work can be lower unless they are a priority. — [Built With Science: best workout split](https://builtwithscience.com/best-workout-split/)
- ACSM 2009 position stand: "higher volume, multiple-set programs are recommended for maximizing hypertrophy"; novices respond to both single and multiple sets. — [ACSM 2009 PDF](https://sfu.ca/~ryand/kin343/ACSMresistance.pdf); [PubMed 19204579](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/19204579/)

### Inferences
- Rule set an AI coach can apply (fractional sets per muscle per week, hard sets near failure): beginner 6-10; intermediate default 10-16; ceiling 20 for most muscles unless the user is specialising and recovering well; never below 4 for any muscle the user wants to grow. For strength on a main lift, 3-6 direct sets/week of that lift (or close variant) captures most of the benefit; extra volume mainly serves hypertrophy.
- Counting rule: direct set = 1.0 to the target muscle; compound set = 0.5 to each synergist (bench/OHP/dips -> 0.5 triceps, 0.5 front delt for bench; rows/pulldowns/chins -> 0.5 biceps, 0.5 rear delt; squats/leg press -> 1 quads, 0.5 glutes; RDL/deadlift -> 1 hamstrings, 0.5-1 glutes, 0.5 lower back/erectors; hip thrust -> 1 glutes, 0.5 hamstrings). This mirrors the Pelland fractional method; exact synergist assignments are coaching convention, not specified in the paper.
- Small muscles (biceps, triceps, side delts, calves) can reach their target with fewer direct sets because of indirect volume (arms) or short recovery demands; large muscles (quads, back, glutes/hamstrings) cost more systemic fatigue per set, so their practical ceiling is often lower in direct sets even though the growth curve looks similar. No meta-analysis shows a different dose-response curve for small vs large muscles.
- Because returns diminish, the biggest win for most users is moving a muscle from <5 to about 10 sets; going from 15 to 25 yields little and costs recovery.

### Gaps
- No primary, verified per-muscle MEV/MAV/MRV table from RP was retrievable; third-party numbers disagree.
- No meta-analysis directly compares dose-response curves between small and large muscles.
- Pelland found little data above about 25 fractional sets/week; upper limits remain uncertain. Moderator analyses (e.g. training status) were described as hypothesis-generating only.

## 2. Training frequency per muscle per week (1x vs 2x vs 3x)

### Takeaway
When weekly volume is equal, frequency has little or no independent effect on hypertrophy; it is a tool to distribute volume. For strength, more frequent practice of a lift helps, with most of the gain from going 1x to 2x per week and accelerating diminishing returns beyond that. Practical default: each muscle 2x/week; 3x is fine and useful when weekly volume is high.

### Cited Findings
- Pelland et al.: frequency and hypertrophy, posterior probability of positive slope 91.3%, credible interval compatible with negligible effects, so no consistent independent effect once volume is controlled. Frequency and strength: 100% posterior probability of positive slope, reciprocal model with diminishing returns. Control-adjusted strength gain rose from 12.72% at 1 session/week to 17.32% at 2 sessions/week; "beyond this point, accelerating diminishing returns occur." — [SportRxiv 460 full text PDF](https://sportrxiv.org/index.php/server/preprint/download/460/967/908)
- Pelland et al. on why higher frequency might matter for strength: more practice of the tested motor pattern; possibly better-quality sessions and heavier loads. They note a likely point where very high frequency impairs recovery (untested). — [SportRxiv 460 full text PDF](https://sportrxiv.org/index.php/server/preprint/download/460/967/908)
- Schoenfeld, Ogborn & Krieger 2016 (Sports Medicine, 10 studies): higher frequency had a larger effect size than lower (0.49 vs 0.30); concluded training each muscle at least twice per week is superior to once. — [Springer PDF s40279-016-0543-8](https://springerlink.bibliotecabuap.elogim.com/content/pdf/10.1007/s40279-016-0543-8.pdf); [Menno Henselmans review](https://mennohenselmans.com/training-frequency-2018-meta-analysis-review/)
- Schoenfeld, Grgic & Krieger 2019 update (J Sports Sci): no significant effect of frequency on hypertrophy in volume-equated studies; in non-equated studies, higher frequency produced more growth (because it allowed more volume). Henselmans criticises that volume was defined as sets x reps. — [Menno Henselmans review](https://mennohenselmans.com/training-frequency-2018-meta-analysis-review/)
- Grgic et al. (frequency and strength meta-regression): no significant relationship in volume-equated studies (p = 0.421), though effect size rose from 1x (ES 0.53) to 2x (ES 0.80). — cited within [SportRxiv 460 full text PDF](https://sportrxiv.org/index.php/server/preprint/download/460/967/908)
- Grgic et al. 2018 trial: 3x vs 6x per week (volume equated) produced similar adaptations in trained men. — [ResearchGate PDF](https://researchgate.net/profile/Jozo-Grgic/publication/327435932_Resistance_Training_Frequencies_of_3_and_6_Times_Per_Week_Produce_Similar_Muscular_Adaptations_in_Resistance-Trained_Men/links/5bd26d6892851c6b278f311d/Resistance-Training-Frequencies-of-3-and-6-Times-Per-Week-Produce-Similar-Muscular-Adaptations-in-Resistance-Trained-Men.pdf)
- ACSM 2009: novices train the whole body 2-3 days/week (Evidence A); intermediates 3-4 days/week (3 if total-body, 4 if upper/lower split "thereby training each major muscle group twice") (Evidence B); advanced 4-6 days/week (Evidence C). 1-2 days/week is an effective maintenance frequency for already-trained people. A cited meta-analysis (Rhea 2003) found 3 days/week best for untrained and 2x/week per muscle best for advanced. — [ACSM 2009 PDF](https://sfu.ca/~ryand/kin343/ACSMresistance.pdf)
- RP's maintenance estimate (about 6 sets/week) assumes at least 2 sessions/week per muscle. — [RP: Training Volume Landmarks](https://rpstrength.com/blogs/articles/training-volume-landmarks-muscle-growth)

### Inferences
- Decision rule: frequency per muscle = ceil(weekly target sets / per-session cap). With a per-session cap of about 8-10 hard sets per muscle (see section 4), up to about 10 sets/week fits 1-2 sessions, 10-20 sets needs 2 sessions, 20+ sets needs 3.
- For users with a strength goal on specific lifts (squat, bench, deadlift, OHP), schedule each main lift or a close variant at least 2x/week, keeping per-session volume moderate.
- 1x/week per muscle is acceptable for maintenance or for muscles that are low priority, not as the default for growth.

### Gaps
- Debate remains over how indirect work counts toward frequency (Pelland used a "fractional" frequency definition, previous meta-analyses differed).
- Little data on frequency effects in highly trained lifters at high volumes.

## 3. Recommended splits per number of training days (2, 3, 4, 5, 6)

### Takeaway
All common splits work if weekly volume per muscle is met and the user can recover and stay consistent. Full body suits 2-3 days, upper/lower suits 4, and 5-6 days suit upper/lower + push/pull/legs hybrids or PPL x2. Each of these gives every muscle about 2x/week; the classic one-muscle-per-day "bro split" gives 1x and is the least efficient choice for beginners and intermediates.

### Cited Findings
- ACSM 2009: novice 2-3 days/week total body; intermediate 3 days total body or 4 days upper/lower (each muscle 2x); advanced 4-6 days with muscle-group splits (1-3 muscle groups per workout). Upper/lower and total-body produced similar strength increases. Also suggests "rotation of upper and lower body or agonist-antagonist exercises" as sequencing options. — [ACSM 2009 PDF](https://sfu.ca/~ryand/kin343/ACSMresistance.pdf)
- Jeremy Ethier: 3-day full body Mon/Wed/Fri (each muscle 3x, recommended for beginners); 4-day upper/lower Mon Upper, Tue Lower, Wed rest, Thu Upper, Fri Lower, weekend rest (2x/week); 6-day PPL Push/Pull/Legs/Push/Pull/Legs with one rest day (2x/week); 5-day bro split gives 1x/week. — [Built With Science: best workout split](https://builtwithscience.com/best-workout-split/)
- StrengthLog describes a 5-day PPLUL hybrid (Push, Pull, Legs, Upper, Lower) that hits each muscle about 2x per week. — [StrengthLog PPLUL](https://www.strengthlog.com/?p=34056); [StrengthLog full body vs split](https://www.strengthlog.com/full-body-vs-split/)
- Garage Gym Reviews: 2x/week full body; 3x/week full body or rotating upper/lower; if one cannot consistently train 6 days, prefer upper/lower or push/pull rather than PPL. — [Garage Gym Reviews: workout splits](https://www.garagegymreviews.com/workout-splits)
- Consensus across coach sources: full body, upper/lower and PPL all work if the lifter enjoys, recovers from and progresses on it. — [Garage Gym Reviews](https://www.garagegymreviews.com/workout-splits); [StrengthLog](https://www.strengthlog.com/full-body-vs-split/)

### Inferences
Concrete templates an AI can apply (my synthesis of the sources above; day orders chosen so the same muscle group is not trained on consecutive days unless noted):
- 2 days: Full body A / Full body B, at least 2 rest days between (e.g. Mon + Thu, or Tue + Fri). Each muscle 2x/week. To reach about 10 sets/muscle, each session needs about 5 sets per major muscle, so sessions are long; prioritise compounds and use supersets for arms/delts.
- 3 days: Full body A/B/C on non-consecutive days (Mon/Wed/Fri). Each muscle 3x/week, about 3-5 sets per muscle per session. Alternative for intermediates: Upper/Lower/Full or rotating Upper/Lower across weeks (each muscle 1.5x/week on average).
- 4 days: Upper/Lower/rest/Upper/Lower (Mon/Tue/Thu/Fri). Each muscle 2x/week; about 5-8 sets per major muscle per session. Upper days can alternate emphasis (A: horizontal push/pull heavy; B: vertical push/pull heavy); lower days alternate squat-focus and hinge-focus.
- 5 days: Upper/Lower/rest/Push/Pull/Legs (PPLUL or ULPPL). Each muscle about 2x/week. Place the rest day after the second consecutive lower-adjacent block; avoid Legs directly after Lower. Example: Mon Upper, Tue Lower, Wed rest, Thu Push, Fri Pull, Sat Legs, Sun rest.
- 6 days: Push/Pull/Legs/Push/Pull/Legs + 1 rest (each muscle 2x/week, 72h+ between repeats of the same day type), or Upper/Lower x3 for lifters wanting more practice of main lifts. Only for users who reliably train 6 days and recover.
- Consecutive-day rule: treat these as overlapping groups: (chest, front delt, triceps), (back, biceps, rear delt), (quads, glutes), (hamstrings, glutes, lower back). Upper then Lower on consecutive days is fine; Push then Pull is fine; two days in a row hitting the same group with hard sets should be avoided except small muscles at low volume (calves, abs, side delts).
- If a user logs fewer days than planned, collapse to the next lower template rather than skipping a day type (e.g. 4-day plan done 3x -> switch to full body so no muscle drops to 1x).

### Gaps
- No primary Stronger by Science or Jeff Nippard split article was retrieved in this pass; coach templates come from Built With Science, StrengthLog and Garage Gym Reviews.
- No controlled trials compare specific day orderings or rest-day placement.

## 4. Session size: exercises and working sets per session, per-muscle per-session cap

### Takeaway
Per-session volume shows diminishing returns: for hypertrophy, additional sets for one muscle in one session stop giving a detectable extra benefit around 11 fractional sets; for strength, around 2 direct sets of the lift per session. A practical cap of about 6-10 hard sets per muscle per session is consistent with this.

### Cited Findings
- Remmert, Pelland, Robinson, Hinson, Zourdos (SportRxiv 537, April 2025), "Is There Too Much of a Good Thing? Meta-Regressions of the Effect of Per-Session Volume on Hypertrophy and Strength": positive dose-response (100% posterior probability) with diminishing returns; point of undetectable outcome superiority (PUOS) about 11 fractional sets per muscle per session for hypertrophy and about 2 direct sets per session for strength. Authors caution data at very high per-session volumes are limited and PUOS is not an optimum. — [SportRxiv 537](https://sportrxiv.org/index.php/server/preprint/view/537); [FAU press release](https://fau.edu/newsdesk/articles/build-muscle-gain-strength-study.php); [EurekAlert](https://www.eurekalert.org/news-releases/1086804)
- Secondary coverage overstates this as "11 sets per session and more than 30 per week are optimal"; that is not what the paper claims. — [BarBend](https://barbend.com/scientifically-correct-number-sets-per-workout)
- Ethier: low end about 3 sets per muscle per session (2 exercises) at 2x/week; high end about 20 sets/week at 3x/week. — [Built With Science](https://builtwithscience.com/best-workout-split/)
- ACSM 2009 exercise order: large before small muscle groups, multi-joint before single-joint, higher intensity before lower intensity; or alternate upper/lower or agonist/antagonist. — [ACSM 2009 PDF](https://sfu.ca/~ryand/kin343/ACSMresistance.pdf)
- ACSM 2009 rest between sets: 3-5 min for heavy strength work; 1-2 min for hypertrophy at moderate loads (6-12RM emphasis). Older adults: 1-3 sets per exercise, 8-12 reps, 60-80% 1RM, 2-3 days/week. — [ACSM 2009 PDF](https://sfu.ca/~ryand/kin343/ACSMresistance.pdf)

### Inferences
- Rule: cap about 8-10 fractional sets per muscle per session (hard cap 11); if the weekly target requires more, add a session for that muscle rather than more sets in one session.
- Session size rule of thumb (my synthesis): 4-7 exercises and about 12-25 working sets per session, 2-4 sets per exercise; full-body sessions use more exercises at fewer sets each (about 2-3 sets), split sessions fewer muscles at more sets.
- For strength-focused main lifts: about 2-5 working sets per session of the main lift, more frequent sessions rather than more sets.

### Gaps
- Per-session analysis is a preprint; peer-reviewed status and version changes (a later version, 689, is referenced) not verified.
- No source found that specifies an optimal total number of exercises per session.

## 5. Recovery between sessions, lower-back fatigue, sleep

### Takeaway
Standard guidance is to train the same muscles on alternating days (about 48h apart) for novices; muscle protein synthesis after a session is elevated for about 36-48h. There is no strong meta-analytic evidence for a precise hours rule; frequency up to 6x/week works when volume per session is moderate. Acute sleep loss measurably blunts muscle protein synthesis.

### Cited Findings
- ACSM 2009: novice frequency of "two to three alternating days per week"; MPS peaks about 24h post-exercise and stays elevated up to 36-48h. — [ACSM 2009 PDF](https://sfu.ca/~ryand/kin343/ACSMresistance.pdf)
- Pelland et al.: MPS is meaningfully reduced 48h after a bout in untrained people and may return to baseline in trained people; yet frequency had no consistent independent hypertrophy effect, possibly because MPS time course does not map directly to hypertrophy (muscle damage repair confounds it). — [SportRxiv 460 full text PDF](https://sportrxiv.org/index.php/server/preprint/download/460/967/908)
- Grgic et al. 2018: 3x and 6x/week (volume equated) produced similar adaptations in trained men, implying daily training of a muscle at low per-session volume is tolerable. — [ResearchGate PDF](https://researchgate.net/profile/Jozo-Grgic/publication/327435932_Resistance_Training_Frequencies_of_3_and_6_Times_Per_Week_Produce_Similar_Muscular_Adaptations_in_Resistance-Trained_Men/links/5bd26d6892851c6b278f311d/Resistance-Training-Frequencies-of-3-and-6-Times-Per-Week-Produce-Similar-Muscular-Adaptations-in-Resistance-Trained-Men.pdf)
- Lamon et al. 2021 (Physiological Reports, n = 13, crossover): one night of total sleep deprivation reduced post-absorptive muscle protein synthesis by 18% (0.072 to 0.059 %/h, p = 0.040), raised cortisol 21% and lowered testosterone (22-24% depending on source); no change in degradation markers. Not a resistance training study. — [UTMB record](https://researchexperts.utmb.edu/en/publications/the-effect-of-acute-sleep-deprivation-on-skeletal-muscle-protein-/); [Deakin figshare](https://deakin.figshare.com/articles/journal_contribution/The_effect_of_acute_sleep_deprivation_on_skeletal_muscle_protein_synthesis_and_the_hormonal_environment/20679646)
- RP: quads carry high systemic fatigue per set (third-party summary). — [Hypertrophy Tower](https://hypertrophytower.pages.dev/hypertrophy/weekly-volume)

### Inferences
- Rule: at least 48h between hard sessions for the same large muscle group (quads, hamstrings/glutes, back, chest); about 72h if the previous session was high volume (8+ sets near failure) or very damaging (heavy eccentrics, new exercises). Small muscles (calves, abs, side delts, forearms) can be trained on consecutive days at low volume.
- Lower back: treat heavy hinges (deadlift, RDL, good morning) and heavy squats/bent rows as drawing on the same erector capacity. Rule of thumb: no heavy hinge on consecutive days; at most one heavy hinge and one heavy squat day per 72h window; place the deadlift at least 48h before the next heavy squat; on the second lower day use a lower-back-sparing hinge (hip thrust, leg curl, back extension at light load) if the first day had heavy deadlifts. This is coaching convention; no direct study found.
- If the user's log shows poor sleep or performance drops on consecutive sessions, the coach should reduce sets by about 20-30% or insert a rest day rather than add volume.

### Gaps
- No controlled study found on lower-back (erector) recovery time between deadlifts, RDLs and squats.
- No study found quantifying how much chronic short sleep reduces training-induced hypertrophy in lifters.

## 6. Abs, calves, forearms, rear delts: direct work needed?

### Takeaway
Evidence here is thin. EMG data show squats and deadlifts activate the rectus abdominis weakly compared with direct ab exercises, so direct ab work is a reasonable addition, but no longitudinal study proves it is required. Rear delts and forearms get substantial indirect work from pulling; calves get little from typical compounds. Coaches treat these as optional/low-priority muscles: about 4-10 direct sets/week if the user wants them to grow.

### Cited Findings
- Bret Contreras (own EMG lab work): most targeted ab/core exercises exceed, sometimes far exceed, squats and deadlifts in abdominal and oblique activation; he notes the lack of longitudinal studies on squats/deadlifts and ab hypertrophy. — [Bret Contreras](https://bretcontreras.com/?p=16968)
- Overhead press and even push-ups produce greater rectus abdominis activity than squats and deadlifts (EMG). — [Cathe](https://cathe.com/are-squats-and-deadlifts-enough-for-your-ab-definition/amp)
- Visible abs depend heavily on body fat level. — [Cathe](https://cathe.com/are-squats-and-deadlifts-enough-for-your-ab-definition/amp)
- Ethier: arms, traps and delts receive indirect volume from compounds; lower direct volume unless a priority. — [Built With Science](https://builtwithscience.com/best-workout-split/)
- Arvo summary of RP: front delts rarely need direct work because pressing covers them. — [Arvo calculator](https://arvo.guru/de/tools/volume-calculator)

### Inferences
- Rules for the coach: abs 0-10 direct sets/week (default about 4-6, 2x/week, can be added to the end of any session); calves 0-12 direct sets/week (default 6-8, 2-3x/week) only if the user cares about calves; rear delts count 0.5 per row/pulldown set and add 4-8 direct sets/week (face pulls, reverse flyes) if pressing volume clearly exceeds pulling; forearms count 0.5 from rows/curls/deadlifts and add direct work only on request.
- Side delts get little from pressing; give 6-12 direct sets/week (lateral raises) for users with physique goals. This follows the small-muscle logic above, not a specific study.

### Gaps
- No longitudinal hypertrophy studies found for abs, calves, rear delts or forearms comparing direct vs indirect work.
- No verified RP per-muscle landmarks for calves, side delts, abs, forearms.

## 7. Specialisation / focus phases for a lagging muscle

### Takeaway
Muscle and strength can be maintained on a fraction of normal volume (as little as 1 session/week and 1 set per exercise, if load stays heavy), which frees recovery capacity to raise volume on one or two lagging muscles.

### Cited Findings
- Spiering et al. 2021 (J Strength Cond Res review): strength and muscle size (at least in younger people) can be maintained for up to 32 weeks with as little as 1 session/week and 1 set per exercise, provided intensity (relative load) is maintained; older adults may need more. — [PubMed 33629972](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/33629972/)
- Bickel et al. 2011: after 16 weeks of training, younger participants maintained muscle on a fraction of their prior volume; secondary sources disagree whether that fraction was about 1/3 or about 1/9. — [Coach Athletics summary](https://coachathletics.com.au/coaching-education/ltad-srbj9); [Look Great Naked](https://www.lookgreatnaked.com/blog/should-you-cut-volume-during-dieting/)
- RP: maintenance volume about 6 sets/muscle/week at 2x/week; MEV is the starting point for a muscle you want to grow. — [RP: Training Volume Landmarks](https://rpstrength.com/blogs/articles/training-volume-landmarks-muscle-growth)
- ACSM 2009: 1-2 days/week is an effective maintenance frequency for trained people. — [ACSM 2009 PDF](https://sfu.ca/~ryand/kin343/ACSMresistance.pdf)

### Inferences
- Specialisation rule for an AI coach: pick at most 1-2 focus muscles per 4-8 week block; raise each to about 15-20 fractional sets/week spread over 3 sessions (per-session cap still about 8-10); drop non-focus muscles to maintenance (about 4-6 sets/week, keep heavy loads, keep at least 1-2 exposures/week); keep total weekly sets roughly unchanged; place focus-muscle work early in sessions when fresh; return to balanced volumes after the block.
- Beginners (under about 6-12 months training) rarely need specialisation; balanced moderate volume grows everything.

### Gaps
- No controlled trials found that directly test specialisation phases (raising one muscle while others are at maintenance) in recreational lifters.
- Bickel 2011 maintenance fraction is reported inconsistently; the primary paper was not retrieved.
