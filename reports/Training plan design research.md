# Volume, effort and stable progression drive good plans

For a general gym-goer the evidence-based weekly plan is simpler than most programs make it look. Give every muscle the lifter wants to grow **about 10 to 16 hard sets per week** (6 to 10 for beginners, rarely above 20), **spread over at least two sessions**, with **no more than about 8 to 10 sets for one muscle in one session**. End sets **1 to 3 reps short of failure**. Use **5 to 10 reps on heavy compounds and 10 to 20 on isolations**, and **keep the main lifts the same for a block** so that a mechanical rule can progress them: add load every session while a beginner still can, then switch to double progression. Frequency, rep range, exercise order and machines versus free weights matter much less for hypertrophy than total hard sets and effort. They matter more for strength on a specific lift. Popular programs (GZCLP, 5/3/1, Reddit PPL, PHUL, Juggernaut, RP) differ mostly in how they write progression and stall rules down, not in this skeleton. The main failure of app-generated plans is randomness: rotating exercises and load suggestions that ignore the lifter's own history. Measured against this, the app's `PLAN_SYSTEM` prompt already gets several hard things right. It keeps the lifter's exercises, rotates sessions by routine rather than calendar, avoids training a muscle hit hard the day before, and estimates new loads cautiously from related lifts. Its gaps are no weekly volume target, no effort (RIR) target, no explicit progression, stall, layoff or deload rule, no split logic by number of days, a single "Legs" bucket that hides quad and hamstring balance, and a single target rep number that cannot express double progression. The final section lists prioritized wording changes for each gap.

**How strong the evidence is.** This report uses one three-level scale throughout. **Strong** means several meta-analyses agree. **Moderate** means one meta-analysis or a handful of controlled trials. **Weak** means expert consensus, practitioner programs or vendor documentation. Many specific numbers that coaches use, such as kg increments, deload percentages, push/pull ratios and lift-to-lift ratios, are weak by this standard. They are presented as coaching convention, not as findings.

## Hard sets per muscle per week are the main lever, with returns diminishing above about 10

The largest dose-response analysis to date (Pelland and colleagues, **67 studies, 2,058 participants**) found that muscle growth keeps rising with weekly sets but with clear diminishing returns. About **4 fractional sets per week is the minimum detectable dose**. **5 to 10 is the most efficient zone**, needing about 6 extra sets per further detectable gain. **11 to 18 is still clearly productive** at about 8.5 extra sets per gain, and **19 to 29 still adds growth but at about 10.75 sets per gain** ([SportRxiv 460](https://sportrxiv.org/index.php/server/preprint/view/460); [full text](https://sportrxiv.org/index.php/server/preprint/download/460/967/908)). Strength plateaus much earlier: beyond about **5 sets per week for a lift, extra sets did not consistently add strength**. The authors found "no clear plateau" for hypertrophy but warned that few studies tested more than about 25 sets. Earlier syntheses agree. Schoenfeld's 2017 dose-response work is commonly summarised as 10 or more weekly sets, and Baz-Valle's 2022 review called **12 to 20 weekly sets** a reasonable standard for trained men ([Baz-Valle 2022](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8884877)). Evidence strength: **strong** for the shape of the curve, **moderate** for the exact tier boundaries, and **weak** above about 25 sets.

How sets are counted matters as much as the target. Pelland tested three ways of counting indirect work and found that **"fractional" counting fit the data best**: a set counts 1.0 for the target muscle and 0.5 for a synergist ([SportRxiv 460](https://sportrxiv.org/index.php/server/preprint/view/460)). A bench press set is one chest set and half a triceps set, and a row is one back set and half a biceps set. Which synergist gets the half is coaching convention, not something the paper specifies. For a coach that reads a log, the practical conclusion is that moving a muscle from under 5 sets to about 10 is the single biggest win. Pushing a muscle from 15 to 25 buys little and costs recovery.

Volume landmarks from Renaissance Periodization (RP) frame the same idea for planning. Maintenance takes **about 6 sets per muscle per week** at two or more sessions, a mesocycle starts near the minimum effective volume, and sets are added week by week toward what the lifter can recover from ([RP volume landmarks](https://rpstrength.com/blogs/articles/training-volume-landmarks-muscle-growth)). RP's own example meso runs **10, 13, 16, then 20 sets** over four weeks before a deload ([RP progressing for hypertrophy](https://rpstrength.com/blogs/articles/progressing-for-hypertrophy)). Per-muscle MEV and MRV tables circulate only through third parties and disagree, so they should not be hard-coded. Evidence strength: **weak** (methodology from one coaching company).

Maintenance is cheap, which makes specialisation possible. A review found that strength and size can be kept **for up to 32 weeks on as little as one session and one set per exercise per week**, if the load stays heavy ([Spiering 2021](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/33629972/)). A lifter with a "muscleFocus" goal can therefore raise one or two muscles toward about 15 to 20 sets while the rest sit near maintenance. Specialisation phases themselves have not been tested directly, so this is a reasonable inference rather than a finding.

Small muscles get much of their work indirectly. Coaches note that arms, traps and front delts receive substantial work from compounds ([Built With Science](https://builtwithscience.com/best-workout-split/)). EMG work shows that **direct ab exercises activate the abs far more than squats and deadlifts**, although no long-term study shows that direct ab work is required ([Bret Contreras](https://bretcontreras.com/?p=16968)). Evidence strength: **weak**. Abs, calves, side delts and rear delts are best treated as optional, low-cost additions of about 4 to 10 direct sets, added when the lifter cares about them.

## Two sessions per muscle, splits matched to the number of days, and a per-session cap

When weekly volume is equal, **frequency has no consistent independent effect on hypertrophy**. Frequency is a way to spread volume. For strength it helps: the control-adjusted strength gain rose from **12.7% at one session per week to 17.3% at two**, with accelerating diminishing returns after that ([Pelland full text](https://sportrxiv.org/index.php/server/preprint/download/460/967/908)). Schoenfeld's 2016 meta-analysis favoured training each muscle at least twice a week ([Schoenfeld 2016](https://springerlink.bibliotecabuap.elogim.com/content/pdf/10.1007/s40279-016-0543-8.pdf)). The 2019 update found that this advantage disappears when volume is equated, which means the benefit of higher frequency comes from the extra volume it allows ([Henselmans review](https://mennohenselmans.com/training-frequency-2018-meta-analysis-review/)). A volume-equated trial of **3 versus 6 sessions per week** in trained men found similar results ([Grgic 2018](https://researchgate.net/profile/Jozo-Grgic/publication/327435932_Resistance_Training_Frequencies_of_3_and_6_Times_Per_Week_Produce_Similar_Muscular_Adaptations_in_Resistance-Trained_Men/links/5bd26d6892851c6b278f311d/Resistance-Training-Frequencies-of-3-and-6-Times-Per-Week-Produce-Similar-Muscular-Adaptations-in-Resistance-Trained-Men.pdf)). Evidence strength: **strong**. Two sessions per muscle is the sensible default because it makes 10 to 20 sets practical, not because frequency builds muscle by itself.

The per-session ceiling follows from the same research group. A per-session meta-regression found the **point beyond which extra sets give no detectable benefit at about 11 fractional sets per muscle per session for hypertrophy, and about 2 direct sets per session for strength** ([Remmert, SportRxiv 537](https://sportrxiv.org/index.php/server/preprint/view/537)). The authors stress that this point is not an optimum, and popular coverage has misread it as "11 sets per session is optimal" ([BarBend](https://barbend.com/scientifically-correct-number-sets-per-workout)). Evidence strength: **moderate**, since it is a single preprint analysis. A cap of about 8 to 10 hard sets per muscle per session is consistent with it. When a weekly target needs more than that, add a session for the muscle instead of more sets in one session.

Splits then follow almost mechanically. ACSM recommends **2 to 3 full-body days for novices** and **3 full-body or 4 upper/lower days for intermediates**, so that each muscle is trained twice ([ACSM 2009](https://sfu.ca/~ryand/kin343/ACSMresistance.pdf)). Coaching sources fill in the higher day counts: upper/lower for 4 days, a push/pull/legs plus upper/lower hybrid for 5, and push/pull/legs twice for 6. All of these train each muscle about twice a week. A one-muscle-per-day "bro split" trains each muscle once and is the least efficient choice ([Built With Science](https://builtwithscience.com/best-workout-split/); [StrengthLog](https://www.strengthlog.com/full-body-vs-split/)). The table below is coaching synthesis, which makes it **weak** evidence as a template. Its frequency logic rests on the **strong** findings above.

| Days | Split | Per-muscle frequency | Typical day order |
|---|---|---|---|
| 2 | Full body A / B | 2x | Mon, Thu |
| 3 | Full body A / B / C (or upper / lower / full body) | 2 to 3x | Mon, Wed, Fri |
| 4 | Upper / lower x2 | 2x | Mon, Tue, Thu, Fri |
| 5 | Upper / lower / push / pull / legs | about 2x | Mon, Tue, Thu, Fri, Sat |
| 6 | Push / pull / legs x2 | 2x | six days, one rest |

Two consequences matter for an AI planner. First, a **3-day push/pull/legs plan trains each muscle only once a week**, and nothing in a naive prompt stops a model from writing it. Second, apps document almost nothing about missed sessions. The robust program-side answer is GZCLP's **"do the next workout in the sequence"** rotation, which shifts the plan when a day is missed instead of breaking it ([GZCLP](https://thefitness.wiki/routines/gzclp/)). Recovery spacing has more physiology than trials behind it. ACSM's novice guidance is "alternating days", based on muscle protein synthesis staying elevated for 36 to 48 hours ([ACSM 2009](https://sfu.ca/~ryand/kin343/ACSMresistance.pdf)). Rules for spacing heavy hinges from heavy squats are coaching convention with no direct study behind them.

## Reps across a wide range, sets stopped one to three reps short, and mechanical progression

Hypertrophy is similar across loads from **about 30% of 1RM upward** when sets end close to failure. Heavy sets of 1 to 5 reps add a modest, partly skill-specific strength advantage ([Schoenfeld 2021, DOAJ](https://doaj.org/article/6d51b905504d46f0873bb43d5d84bf21); [abstract](https://www.x-mol.com/paper/1363971354706350080)). Moderate ranges are a choice of time efficiency and comfort, not a growth requirement. Evidence strength: **strong** for "similar growth across loads". The practical split of about 5 to 10 reps for heavy compounds and 10 to 20 for isolations comes from practitioner guidance ([Arvo RP summary](https://arvo.guru/resources/methods/rp-training)), so it is **weak** as a specific prescription. It is still sensible, because a lateral raise with 2 kg dumbbell jumps needs a higher, wider range than a squat.

Effort is the other half of the stimulus. A meta-analysis of 15 studies found that **training to failure had only a trivial advantage** over stopping short (ES 0.19), and no advantage when failure was defined strictly ([Refalo 2023](https://dro.deakin.edu.au/articles/journal_contribution/Influence_of_Resistance_Training_Proximity-to-Failure_on_Skeletal_Muscle_Hypertrophy_A_Systematic_Review_with_Meta-analysis/22030796)). A meta-regression over **55 studies** showed hypertrophy improving as sets end closer to failure, while strength stayed flat across a wide range of reps in reserve ([Robinson 2024](https://rke.abertay.ac.uk/en/publications/exploring-the-dose-response-relationship-between-estimated-resist/)). In a within-subject trial, quadriceps growth was **identical (0.181 versus 0.182 cm)** whether leg press and leg extension went to failure or stopped at 1 to 2 RIR, but failure caused more fatigue ([Refalo 2024](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/38393985/)). Evidence strength: **strong**. Lifters also misjudge RIR by **about one rep**, and they judge better near failure and in sets of 12 reps or fewer ([Halperin 2022](https://sportrxiv.org/index.php/server/preprint/view/67)). The coaching translation is about 1 to 3 RIR on heavy free-weight compounds and 0 to 2 on machines and isolations. Never plan true failure on squats or deadlifts. That split between compounds and isolations is practitioner consensus.

Progression rules in proven programs are explicit and boring. Beginner routines **add load every session**, typically +2.5 lb on upper-body lifts and +5 lb on squat and deadlift. The r/fitness Basic Beginner Routine handles stalls with a **10% drop** when the lifter falls below 15 total reps ([Basic Beginner Routine](https://thefitness.wiki/routines/r-fitness-basic-beginner-routine/)). GZCLP handles misses by changing rep stages at the same load (5x3, then 6x2, then 10x1) before resetting to **85% of a new 5RM** ([GZCLP](https://thefitness.wiki/routines/gzclp/)). Reddit PPL runs accessories on **double progression**: start at the bottom of the range, add reps each session, and add load at the top ([Liftosaur PPL](https://www.liftosaur.com/programs/metallicadpappl)). 5/3/1 moves a training max set at 90% of 1RM by fixed amounts per three-week cycle ([5/3/1 for Beginners](https://thefitness.wiki/routines/5-3-1-for-beginners/)). Across all of them, **lower-body increments are about twice the upper-body ones**. Starting Strength resets loads **5 to 10% after a layoff, and 10% after 3 to 4 weeks off** ([Starting Strength](https://mail.startingstrength.com/article/the-reset-why-and-how)). RPE-based load selection matched or slightly beat %1RM loading in trained men ([Helms 2018](https://www.frontiersin.org/articles/10.3389/fphys.2018.00247/text)), which supports letting effort feedback steer loads when it is available. Evidence strength: **weak** for any specific increment or stall ladder, because these are program conventions. They are also battle-tested, and their value for an AI coach lies precisely in being fixed rules instead of fresh judgement each time.

Deload evidence is thin. A Delphi consensus agreed that a deload cuts sets, reps, days and proximity to failure, and that pre-planned, reactive or combined timing are all acceptable. It reached **no consensus on duration or percentage cuts** ([Bell 2023](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10511399/)). Surveyed strength and physique athletes deload **every 5.6 ± 2.3 weeks for about 6 days**, and **78.9% cut weekly sets** ([Bell 2024](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10948666/)). A full week off in the middle of a block left hypertrophy unchanged but slightly favoured continuous training for squat strength, with a posterior probability of 0.85 ([Coleman 2024](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10809978/)). Dr. Muscle's automatic deload, triggered by a plateau or a drop in estimated 1RM, **cuts sets by 50% and weights by 10%** ([Dr. Muscle](https://dr-muscle.com/building-muscle/)). Evidence strength: **weak to moderate**. A reduced-volume week at the same exercises, triggered by stalls or offered every 4 to 8 weeks for intermediates, is a defensible default. A week off is not.

Starting weights for a new exercise have essentially **no research behind them**. The e1RM formulas (Epley, Brzycki) agree for sets of 1 to 10 reps and lose accuracy above that. All equations tested in one study **overestimated leg press 1RM by 6 to 15%** ([RBAFS](https://rbafs.org.br/RBAFS/article/download/2400/pdf47/3542); [PMC9465738](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9465738)). Dumbbell bench per hand runs at **about 40 to 45% of barbell bench** ([FitnessVolt](https://fitnessvolt.com/strength-standards/compare/bench-press-vs-dumbbell-bench-press/)). Other lift-to-lift ratio tables are unsourced. Fitbod states that it **starts conservatively** for unlogged exercises ([Fitbod help](https://help.fitbod.me/hc/en-us/articles/360004429814-How-Fitbod-Creates-Your-Workout)). Users still report wild suggestions such as **50 kg rows for someone who rows 20 kg** ([Dr. Muscle on Fitbod](https://dr-muscle.com/fitbod-app-review-alternative/)). The defensible approach is an estimate from a related lift biased low, a first-session target of about 3 RIR, and a load-up within the session. When nothing related exists, the lifter finds the working weight by ramping.

Beginners differ from intermediates mainly in how fast they progress and what they need. Beginners progress session to session for weeks or months and rarely need planned deloads. Intermediates progress weekly or per cycle and benefit from rep ranges, effort targets and deloads ([Barbell Logic](https://barbell-logic.com/linear-progression-the-simple-way-to-strength/); [Basic Beginner Routine](https://thefitness.wiki/routines/r-fitness-basic-beginner-routine/)). No retrieved source quantifies typical weekly gains by training age. A log-reading coach can detect the level per lift: if the lifter still adds load every session, treat the lift as linear, and once it has stalled repeatedly, switch it to double progression.

## Session design: stable lifts, compounds first, long-length accessories, antagonist supersets

Keep exercises stable. In a volume-equated trial, a group whose exercises were randomised by an app each session gained **bench 1RM +0.77% versus +4.7%** for a group with fixed exercises, and grew the rectus femoris less (+0.05 versus +0.18 cm). The random group did report **higher motivation (ES 1.28)** ([Baz-Valle 2019](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6934277/)). A systematic review concluded that some systematic variation helps, while **excessive random variation compromises gains** ([Kassiano 2022](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/35438660/)). Evidence strength: **moderate**. The pattern matches the most common user complaint about generator apps ([Dr. Muscle on Fitbod](https://dr-muscle.com/fitbod-app-review-alternative/)). The resulting rule is to keep the first one or two lifts of each session fixed for a block, rotate accessories only between blocks or on request, and rotate to a meaningfully different angle rather than a near-duplicate.

Exercise order mostly affects strength on whatever comes first. A meta-analysis found that strength gains on multi-joint lifts favoured doing them first (**ES 0.32**) and found **no order effect on hypertrophy** ([Nunes 2021](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/32077380/)). ACSM's "large before small, multi-joint before single-joint" rule is evidence category C ([ACSM 2009](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/19204579/)). Evidence strength: **moderate**. Compounds first is a good default, and a lift the lifter has a strength goal for belongs early in the session.

Exercise choice shapes regional growth. Overhead triceps extensions grew the long head more than pushdowns ([Maeo 2022](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/35819335/)). **Seated leg curls grew the hamstrings about 14% versus about 9% for prone curls** ([Maeo 2021](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/33009197/)). Leg extensions grow the rectus femoris, which squats barely touch ([SportRxiv 407](https://doi.org/10.51224/SRXIV.407)). Squats grew the glutes about as much as hip thrusts did, and grew the quads and adductors more ([Plotkin 2023](https://www.pubmed.gov/37877099/)). A meta-analysis found a small edge for lengthened partials over full range of motion (ES 0.28) ([Wolf 2023](https://journal.iusca.org/index.php/Journal/article/view/182)), but newer trained-lifter data show parity ([PeerJ 2025](https://peerj.com/articles/18904)). Evidence strength: **moderate**. Each muscle is best covered by two or more exercises at different joint angles across the week, with at least one loading it at a long muscle length. For legs specifically, a squat-pattern lift alone leaves the hamstrings' knee-flexion role untrained. No study sets a push:pull ratio. Practitioners recommend pulling volume at least equal to pressing ([Muscle & Strength](https://www.muscleandstrength.com/articles/no-pain-shoulder-superset)), which is **weak** evidence. Machines and free weights give **equivalent hypertrophy**, while strength transfers best to the modality that was trained ([Haugen 2023](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC10426227/)).

Supersets are a legitimate time tool. A 2025 meta-analysis found sessions **about 37% shorter** with similar hypertrophy and 1RM gains. Agonist-antagonist pairs produced more total reps, same-muscle pairs **lowered volume load (SMD -1.08)**, and perceived exertion rose ([Zhang 2025](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12011898/)). A trial in 43 trained adults found **36% shorter sessions** with similar growth ([Burke 2025](https://sportrxiv.org/index.php/server/preprint/view/419)). Evidence strength: **moderate**, because only three of the meta-analysis studies were long-term. Rest affects hypertrophy in a small way. A Bayesian meta-analysis found a **small benefit for rest above 60 seconds and nothing extra beyond 90 seconds** ([Singer 2024](https://frontiersin.org/journals/sports-and-active-living/articles/10.3389/fspor.2024.1429789/abstract)). On heavy lifts, **3 minutes beat 1 minute for strength** ([Schoenfeld 2016](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/26605807/)). Warm-ups matter mainly for heavy compounds. One or two progressive sets, up to about 80% of the working load, improved squat and bench performance ([Ribeiro 2020](https://doaj.org/article/90de36e35e514c36af5f61118a42eb3a)).

## Programs share one skeleton, while apps split into generators and planners

Popular programs look different on paper but share a skeleton. Each session has **one or two tracked main lifts on a mechanical rule**, then a secondary compound at more reps, then accessories at 8 to 15 or more reps on double progression. A capped last set (AMRAP at 1 to 2 RIR, or an RIR target) serves as the feedback signal. GZCLP makes this explicit as tiers: **T1 at 5x3+ with 3 to 5 minutes rest, T2 at 3x10 with 2 to 3 minutes, and T3 at 3x15+ with 1 to 1.5 minutes**, rotated through four workouts over three days a week ([GZCLP](https://thefitness.wiki/routines/gzclp/)). 5/3/1 for Beginners pairs percentage work on a training max with "First Set Last" volume and **50 to 100 reps each of push, pull and single-leg or core assistance** ([5/3/1 for Beginners](https://thefitness.wiki/routines/5-3-1-for-beginners/)). PHUL runs four upper/lower days, two "power" days at **3 to 4 x 3 to 5** on main compounds and two "hypertrophy" days at **3 to 4 x 8 to 12**, with no planned deload or failure rule ([Setgraph PHUL](https://setgraph.app/ai-blog/phul-workout-split-guide); [Legion PHUL](https://legionathletics.com/phul-workout)). Juggernaut runs **four-week waves of 10s, 8s, 5s and 3s**, each ending in an all-out set that moves the max by a fixed amount per extra rep, plus a deload every fourth week ([Liftosaur Juggernaut](https://www.liftosaur.com/programs/juggernaut-method)). Stronger by Science's programs adjust the training max weekly from AMRAP or RIR results ([Lift Vault SBS](https://liftvault.com/programs/strength/stronger-by-science-sbs-program-bundle-by-greg-nuckols/)). RP's hypertrophy model adds sets across a 4 to 6 week meso, moves from higher to lower RIR, and ends the meso with a deload ([RP progressing for hypertrophy](https://rpstrength.com/blogs/articles/progressing-for-hypertrophy); [Physique Collective](https://physiquecollective.com/extras/rphypertrophyapp)). Jeff Nippard's Fundamentals comes in 3-day full body, 4-day upper/lower and 5-day body-part versions with per-exercise RPE targets ([Jeff Nippard](https://jeffnippard.com/product/fundamentals-hypertrophy-program)). These programs have earned their status through community use, not head-to-head trials, so the skeleton is **weak** evidence on its own. It matches the stronger findings above on volume, effort and stability, though.

Apps fall into two families. **Recovery-driven generators**, chiefly Fitbod, score each muscle on a **0 to 100% recovery scale**, pick recovered muscles and pick exercises from cross-user effectiveness data and preferences. They also deliberately vary sets, reps and loads from an estimated 1RM ([Fitbod help](https://help.fitbod.me/hc/en-us/articles/360004429814-How-Fitbod-Creates-Your-Workout); [Fitbod algorithm](https://fitbod.me/blog/fitbod-algorithm)). **Program-driven planners** keep a fixed split or meso and adjust only load, reps and sets. RP Hypertrophy sets next week's volume from post-session pump, soreness, joint pain and workload ratings ([RP app](https://rpstrength.com/hypertrophy-app)). JuggernautAI adjusts through loops at pre-session readiness, within the session from top-set RPE, and at the end of each session, week and block ([JuggernautAI help](https://help.jtsstrength.com/en/articles/3-how-juggernautai-is-individualized-to-you)). Alpha Progression recommends each set from previous performance and available weights using double progression, with RIR optional ([Alpha Progression guide](https://alphaprogression.com/en/blog/alpha-progression-guide)). Dr. Muscle auto-deloads when estimated 1RM stalls or drops ([Dr. Muscle](https://dr-muscle.com/building-muscle/)). User complaints concentrate on the generator family's **randomness and unrealistic loads**, and on the planner family's **over-reaction to poorly calibrated feedback** and rigid meso lengths ([Dr. Muscle on Fitbod](https://dr-muscle.com/fitbod-app-review-alternative/); [Physique Collective](https://physiquecollective.com/extras/rphypertrophyapp); [aitoolsbakery on JuggernautAI](https://aitoolsbakery.com/blog/juggernautai-review/)). Most of these reports come through competitor review sites. None of the retrieved app documentation describes a rule for missed sessions, and JuggernautAI's help page is explicitly silent on it. That gap is real, and the "next workout in the sequence" rotation is the best available answer. The app's coach is already a program-driven planner in this sense, with the LLM's judgement standing in for the written rules that the other planners keep in code.

## The current coach prompt: solid foundations, missing dose, effort and progression rules

### What the model receives today

`planningLog` sends `recent`, which covers the current week plus four earlier weeks (`CONTEXT_WEEKS = 4`), with each set as "reps x kg", warm-ups marked "W", per-exercise targets ("sets x reps"), superset numbers, and workout, exercise and set notes. It sends `muscleGroups` with exact working-set counts per group for this week and the four earlier weeks, a last-trained date and a "skippedOnPurpose" flag, where each exercise counts for its single main group only. It also sends `goals` (lift and muscle-focus goals), `planned` (the next 14 days of scheduled workouts, which `suggestWeek` deliberately blanks), `preferences` (preferred weekdays and a free-text coach note) and a `catalog` of id, name, main muscle group and a bodyweight flag. The standard groups are Chest, Back, Shoulders, Biceps, Triceps, Legs, Glutes and Abs, so quads, hamstrings and calves share one "Legs" bucket. The model does **not** receive any effort signal (RIR, RPE or session rating) apart from free-text notes. It also gets no training age or experience level, no strength-versus-size goal type, no session time budget, no equipment type or load increments per exercise, no bodyweight, no secondary muscles per exercise, no weeks since the last deload, and no history older than five weeks.

### What it already does well

Several of the prompt's choices line up directly with the evidence. "Keep their usual exercises in their usual order, with their sets, reps and way of progressing" is exactly what Baz-Valle 2019 and the Fitbod complaints argue for. Picking "which session is due in their routine" instead of a calendar slot mirrors GZCLP's rotation, the most robust known answer to missed days. "Do not plan a muscle group that was trained hard yesterday or today" matches the 48-hour guidance. The week task's "the large ones (back, chest, legs, shoulders) preferably twice" captures the strongest frequency finding. Compounds get 5 to 10 reps and isolations 10 to 15, consistent with the practitioner split. Starting weights are estimated from related lifts and kept light so the lifter "can go up within the session", which is the best available practice. Not supersetting heavy compounds is a defensible safety and logistics caution. The evidence would allow bench plus row at moderate loads, but nothing is lost by being conservative there. Server-side code does what models do badly: it checks catalog ids, normalises supersets and flags estimated weights.

### What is missing or in tension with the evidence

The prompt never states a **weekly set target per muscle**. The model sees exact counts but has no rule for what is too little or too much, so it cannot tell 4 sets from 14. It has **no effort target**, although proximity to failure is one of the two strongest variables. It gives **no explicit progression, stall, layoff or deload rule**, only "continue the lifter's own progression", so every suggestion depends on the model's improvisation. That is the failure mode the program literature avoids by writing rules down. It has **no split logic by day count**, so a 3-day push/pull/legs week (each muscle trained once) is allowed. It has **no per-session cap** per muscle. It says nothing about **indirect volume**, even though the analysis prompt `SYSTEM` does. The **"Legs" bucket** can show 12 sets that are all quads. There are also two internal inconsistencies. The prompt says "5 to 6 exercises" while the `PlannedWorkoutSchema` description says "Five to seven". And `reps` is a single number, which cannot express the range that double progression needs. The prompt also has **no sanity cap** on suggested loads, and `checkPlan` only clamps weights to 2000 kg. The prompt does not mention long-length exercises or push/pull balance, and it does not put a goal lift first in the session. Finally, with no time budget the model cannot decide when antagonist supersets would actually help.

### Inputs that would help most

The cheapest high-value addition is an **optional RIR or "how hard" value on the last set of each exercise**, or failing that, a **one-tap session rating** (too easy, about right, too hard), as Dr. Muscle uses ([Dr. Muscle](https://dr-muscle.com/what-makes-dr-muscle-different/)). Without it the model can only infer effort from rep drop-off at the same load. Alpha Progression shows that a planner works without RIR, which is why it should stay optional ([Alpha Progression](https://alphaprogression.com/en/blog/reps-in-reserve)). Rich multi-scale feedback, such as RP's pump, soreness and joint ratings, has been reported to **over-correct volume when users misjudge the scales** ([Physique Collective](https://physiquecollective.com/extras/rphypertrophyapp)). Any new signal should therefore move the plan by at most one set or one load step per session. A **joint pain flag per exercise** would let the coach swap exercises for a reason the log cannot show. Beyond feedback, the most useful additions are an **experience level** (or the server-computed age of the oldest logged workout) so beginner and intermediate rules can apply, a **primary goal** (strength, size or general) so rep ranges and effort can shift for strength-focused lifters, a **session time budget**, an **equipment type per catalog exercise** (barbell, dumbbell, machine, cable) so load steps are not guessed from names, the lifter's **bodyweight** for bodyweight exercises, and **weeks since the last lighter week**. The most leverage per token comes from **server-computed trend fields per exercise**, such as the best e1RM from sets of 10 reps or fewer and the number of sessions since the weight or reps last went up. These let the model apply the stall rules from numbers instead of counting across the whole log.

## Conclusion

The research shifts the coach's job from choosing exercises to managing a few dials with fixed rules. Weekly hard sets per muscle, effort per set and a written progression ladder do most of the work. Splits, frequency, rep ranges and exercise order are largely ways of delivering that dose within the lifter's calendar and equipment. This favours the app's current design instincts (stable exercises, routine-based rotation, conservative estimates), because the biggest risk of an LLM planner is the same randomness that made generator apps unpopular. It also means the prompt should constrain the model more, not less: explicit numeric targets and stall rules make suggestions predictable enough for the lifter to trust and for the server to verify.

The weakest links in the evidence sit exactly where an AI coach acts most often: starting weights for new exercises, load increments, deload timing and how to read effort from a log. None of these has strong trial support. The honest design response is to use conservative program conventions, cap changes per session, and collect the one signal that would make the inference much better, namely how hard the last set felt. A separate, larger structural fix is splitting "Legs" and giving catalog exercises secondary muscles. Both are data-model changes rather than prompt edits, and they would let the server compute the fractional volume that the best evidence says is the right way to count.
